A card

For Rhett

From Wil, May 2026

Thomas Jefferson wrote a letter to two college grads in preparation for their "Grand Tour" of Europe. This hastily written, deeply opinionated travel itinerary is disguised as a "curriculum" on republican citizenship. You'll see the three layers of structure, logistics, sight-seeing, and America-in-Europe, but it's my understanding that Jefferson intended this as a learning-objectives document for any future traveler.

Throughout the letter, he talks about his own regrets and lessons learned through his 45 years as a farmer, student, aristocrat, philosopher, musician, revolutionary, statesman, diplomat, and ambassador. Even though he wrote it to these two grads, he kept a copy for himself. I imagine subconsciously that this was so that future travelers could have this same advice.

A lot has changed in the last 240 years (some of the princes he recommended to see in Holland might not be there anymore), but I wrote up a modernized version for you. I hoped to capture his original voice and advice while framing it around something relevant for you. Instead of focusing on what political ideologies to bring back home, I wrote it as a guide for capturing the uncapturable.

Jefferson is also writing from a very specific place in his own life: a wealthy slaveholding Virginian who has fallen for European aesthetics (architecture, wine, gardens) while remaining deeply suspicious of European institutions (courts, monarchy, luxury). That tension drives his whole life. The institution of slavery vs the aesthetic of Monticello; the institution of government vs the aesthetic of a republic; the institution of education vs the aesthetic of W&M.

Aside from the obvious W&M connection, there's a lot of you that I see in him. The institution vs aesthetics conflict certainly was difficult for Jefferson, but it was also the reason why he was so thoughtful, considerate, and deliberate. Not in the emotional way, but in the sense where he would consider and think through different scenarios from a source of self-understanding.

Many times this forced him into a spiral. He got caught up on what the "right" answer was, what the "right" way to present himself was, what the "right" way to travel was. The "aesthetic" that Jefferson continually sought out was an idealistic version of society modeled after himself.

It would not surprise me to see him return an iPhone 15 for an iPhone 13, or road trip across America with a younger John Adams before going to W&M. His aesthetic of Monticello is a little different from yours as a cabin in the woods, but the self-sufficient, self-reliant yeoman farmer was Jefferson's dream for us.

He was a radical guy for thinking outside the box. When you go on this trip, you're going to experience things from so many different angles. Apertures, if you will. You know better than I do that photos can somehow capture a memory or a story while also being a copy of an irreplaceable experience. Don't get caught up thinking about what you're supposed to do, where you're supposed to go, and what you're supposed to take pictures of. Don't limit what counts as "interesting" enough to capture.

Jefferson had his own version of a camera. Like many 18th-century aristocrats, he obsessively tracked distances and prices, and filled notebooks with what he wanted to remember. While I'm sure you won't get much use surveying the lengths of the streets in Amsterdam, I got you this measurement tool for photography.

While you're out there, remember that you are your experiences. Capture the moments and find your own meaning in a part of the world that is not yours. Jefferson's Europe was an ocean and three months away from home; yours is a phone call. You shouldn't need the distance finder to remember where you came from. Use it to measure where you're going.
