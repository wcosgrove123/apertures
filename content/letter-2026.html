A modernized letter

Hints to a Young American Travelling in Europe

Modernized from Thomas Jefferson's letter to Shippen and Rutledge, June 1788

Money and Setup

Carry €100 in cash for the first day. After that you will rarely touch it. Apply for a Charles Schwab debit card before you leave. It has no foreign ATM fees and reimburses any the machine charges, which over a few months adds up to a full week's lodging. A no-foreign-transaction credit card (Capital One Venture, Chase Sapphire, anything similar) for everything else. Set up Wise before you go. Notify both banks of your departure dates or they will lock the cards the first time they see Amsterdam, and the call to Bank of America from a hostel in the Jordaan is one I would spare you.

Buy a SIM or eSIM at the airport. T-Mobile claims to give you international data for free, and it does, at speeds barely sufficient to load a map.

A small notebook. The names of the people you meet, the prices of things, the words you hear. You will not remember them otherwise.

Learn enough of each language to ask where things are and how much they cost. Jefferson once took a back road into Westphalia on purpose, to see the battlefield where Varus lost his legions, and could find no one in the village who spoke a language he spoke. He missed what he had come for. Do not make his mistake.

Before leaving, ask anyone you know for letters of introduction to friends or relations in these cities. Present yourself on arrival. The kitchen of a stranger who knows the city is better than any restaurant in it.

Amsterdam

A good place to begin. Buy a transit card on arrival; you can tap your credit card on the metro now, but the local card is faster. I lodged near the Jordaan and it was the best decision of the trip. The cafes are slower, the canals less photographed, and you can walk to everything that matters. Café 't Smalle for an afternoon coffee. The Rijksmuseum is worth a half-day, no more. Vermeer's Milkmaid is small and the room is crowded; go anyway. Remark the light in Holland, particularly in October and early spring. It is the same light those painters were chasing.

For street work, the Albert Cuyp market on weekday mornings, the canals around Brouwersgracht near sundown, and Centraal Station at any hour. Avoid the red-light district as a photographic subject. Not for moral reasons. For craft reasons. It has been done by every photographer who came through this city in the last forty years, and the women there are not props.

Berlin

The Rhine that Jefferson floated down is now the German rail network, and the DB Navigator app is your post-coach. Look into the Deutschland-Ticket (around €60 a month, verify before buying) if you plan to stay more than two weeks; it covers regional trains and local transit in every city except for high-speed ICE service.

Berlin is the city where the twentieth century is still legible in the streets. Stay in Neukölln or Kreuzberg, not Mitte. Walk the East Side Gallery once and then never again. The best museum in Berlin is the streets themselves. If you must do one indoors, the Berlinische Galerie has excellent photography, and the Hamburger Bahnhof has the best contemporary collection. The Pergamon Museum is currently closed for renovation; check before you go.

For photo books, do you read me?! in Mitte. Bring an empty bag.

A warning. Berlin has been the subject of more bad street photography than any other city in Europe. The temptation to make every frame into a meditation on the Wall is strong. Resist. The city is alive now. Photograph what is here.

Prague or Vienna

I will not tell you which. Prague is cheaper, the beer is better, and the old town is the most cinematic place in central Europe. Vienna is denser and sharper, harder to love but easier to admire. Both are reachable from Berlin by overnight train. Take whichever route gets you to Italy by way of a place you have not been.

If Prague, the Letná park at sunset, the trams crossing the Charles Bridge at dawn, and the produce markets in Holešovice. If Vienna, the Beisl (neighborhood taverns) for dinner, the U-Bahn at rush hour for portraits, and the Schiele room at the Leopold for one good hour.

Florence

Stay one neighborhood away from the Duomo. The Oltrarno, the south side of the river, is where Florentines actually live, and where the leather workshops and bookbinders still operate at workbenches you can see through open doors. The street photography in Florence is in those doorways and in the markets at San Lorenzo and Sant'Ambrogio at seven in the morning.

The Uffizi has timed entry and you will need to book a week ahead. The Bargello, two blocks away, has the best Donatellos in the world and almost no line. Worth an afternoon.

Eat where the menu is on a chalkboard and changes daily. Trattoria Mario at lunch is correct. All'Antico Vinaio for a panino if you can stand the line.

Rome

Stay in Trastevere or Monti, not near the Vatican or Termini. Walk from the Pantheon to Campo de' Fiori to Piazza Navona in the first hour of evening light. Do this twice. Once for orientation. Once with your camera. The travertine glows at that hour. The afternoon light is harsh and flat.

The Vatican Museums are best entered at 7:30am with a pre-booked ticket. By 10am the corridors are unphotographable. The Sistine Chapel is overrated relative to the Raphael Rooms two galleries before it. You may not take pictures in the Sistine; do not even try. Photograph the people queuing instead.

Da Enzo al 29 in Trastevere if you can get in. The amatriciana at Roscioli is correct.

Naples

If you have one city in Europe for street photography, it is this one. Stay in the Spanish Quarters or Sanità. Naples is loud, fast, beautiful, and pushed back. The light is hard. The people will tell you to put the camera down or to come closer, often within the same minute. Both responses are right, and the city will teach you which one is right when.

Pizza at Da Michele on Via Cesare Sersale, and at Sorbillo, and decide for yourself. Do not be persuaded to take the day trip to Capri. Capri is for the wealthy. Procida is for the rest.

Through Provence

Take the TGV from Italy down to Nice. Skip the Italian Riviera; Jefferson took mules across 125 miles of it and complained about every one of them. Nice is fine. Marseille is more interesting.

The real reason to come south is Provence. Stay in Arles for a week if you can. The Rencontres d'Arles, the international photography festival, runs every July; if you can time your visit to it, you should. Arles is the city Van Gogh painted, and the light is exactly what he saw. The Roman amphitheater is still in use. The Cafe Van Gogh on the Place du Forum is a tourist trap, but the building is real and the light at 7pm has not changed.

Avignon, the Pont du Gard, and Nîmes can be done in a single day if you must, but better to give each a morning. The Maison Carrée at Nîmes is the building Jefferson copied for the Virginia State Capitol. This is, in a real sense, the building that gave America its civic vocabulary. Worth an hour.

Paris

Last. Always last. Paris is the city you end in because every city before it has been training your eye for it.

Stay in the 11th or the 20th, not in the 1st through the 7th. The center is for tourists. The east is where Paris lives. Remark which neighborhoods have benches and which do not. It tells you who the city is being built for. The Marais on a Sunday morning. Belleville on a Sunday afternoon. Père Lachaise any day. The Promenade Plantée. The Canal Saint-Martin. The bouquinistes along the Seine.

For photography, the Jeu de Paume and the Maison Européenne de la Photographie. The Louvre is enormous; pick one wing and stay all day. The Mona Lisa is not the painting in the Louvre. Find your own painting.

I made one mistake in Paris that I want you to avoid. I tried to photograph the Eiffel Tower from every angle in a single afternoon. It cannot be done, and the resulting images are exactly as good as the ten thousand you have already seen. Photograph the tower in passing, as something that happens to be there, not as a subject.

General Observations

Buy Lonely Planet The World before you leave, and the map of any country you are going into. On arriving in any new town, buy the plan of the town and the book noting its curiosities. Walk the perimeter when there is one. Go to the top of a tower or a hill to have a view of the town and its environs. The internet will tempt you to do without the printed map and book; do not be persuaded.

When you are deciding whether something is worth the trip, remember that you will not be this close to it again. You may regret not seeing a thing. You will not regret seeing one. But there is the opposite extreme. Do not try to see everything. The Louvre will defeat you. The Vatican will defeat you. Choose three rooms and stay long enough to remember them. Take particular care not to let the audio guides, the upgraded tour packages, and the skip-the-line bundles lead you through the trifles in their possession. They want your money, and suppose you give it more willingly the more they detail to you.

When you order wine in a place you do not know, ask for what the locals drink, not what is on the menu. The house wine is usually local, cheap, and good. The "imported selection" is usually a markup on something you would not buy at home.

The people you will see most often are hostel staff, baristas, train conductors, and rideshare drivers. These are the same in every city in the world, and they are not the country. Do not form your opinion of a place from them.

Before entering each country, study the photographers who have worked there. Cartier-Bresson before Paris. Koudelka before Prague. Giacomelli before central Italy. Your own choices for the rest. It gives infinite pleasure to apply one's reading on the spot. Besides, it aids your future recollection of the place.

Buy a Eurail pass before you arrive, the latest edition. It is the post book of Europe.

The markets, the parks, the trains at rush hour, the squares at sundown to be frequented. At these you see the inhabitants from high to low.

Objects of Attention for an American with a Camera

1

Agriculture

Everything belonging to this art, and whatever has a near relation to it. Photograph the people who work the land. The vineyards of the Moselle, the olive presses of Provence, the rice fields of Vercelli, the cheesemakers of Rozzano. Photograph also the animals and the seasons. New methods which might be brought home in your notebook, if not in your camera.

2

Mechanical arts

So far as they respect things necessary in America, and inconvenient to be transported thither ready made. Photograph the forges, the stone quarries, the boats, the bridges (very specially). Photograph also the people who build them and the people who maintain them. The work that holds the cities up is not the work the cities advertise.

3

Lighter mechanical arts and manufactures

Jefferson insisted America would never become a manufacturing nation. He saw the world through his idealistic vision, and advised not to waste time studying European factories. My own advice here is to capture and experience things you would not expect to enjoy. Create a story from what you believe to be story-less. Seek out opportunities to expand what counts as "interesting."

4

Gardens

Peculiarly worth the attention of an American, because it is the country of all others where the noblest gardens may yet be made. Photograph the formal gardens of Versailles and Schwetzingen, the English-style grounds of Lombardy, the kitchen gardens behind every Italian house. The composition of a garden teaches the composition of a frame.

5

Architecture

Worth great attention. As America doubles its numbers every twenty years it must double its houses, and we build of such perishable materials that half of what stands today will be rebuilt within twenty years. Photograph everything: the Roman temples that survived two thousand years, the Gothic cathedrals that survived eight hundred, the Bauhaus blocks that survived a century, the postwar towers already coming down. It is among the most important arts, and your photographs are the record of which choices held.

6

Painting, statuary

Too established for you to add to through the camera. It would be useless and preposterous to endeavor to make yourself a connoisseur in those arts by photographing them. Photograph them in their context: the painting on the gallery wall with the visitors before it, the statue in the square with the pigeons on it. They are worth seeing, but not studying.

7

Politics of each country

Well worth studying, and especially worth photographing, so far as respects internal affairs. Examine their influence on the happiness of the people. Take every possible occasion of entering the kitchens of the labourers, and especially at the moments of their repast, see what they eat, how they are clothed, whether they are obliged to labour too hard, whether the government or their landlord takes from them an unjust proportion of their labour, on what footing stands the property they call their own, their personal liberty. Photograph all of it. Each is a life. This is the work of a lifetime, and the lifetime begins here.

8

Courts

Jefferson's last point is a fiery attack on the courts, which he compares to wild animals and "humanity's weakest part." Ironically, his judgment uses the same logic he critiques in the courts themselves. If you come across a court in your travels, I advise not to think about the political infrastructure or lobbyist influence. Let it instead be a reminder of the liberty you have, protected. Think of the freedom you have to travel and explore the world. The same liberty Jefferson had when he crossed an ocean to Europe, and that you have now, to photograph fishmongers in Naples. Photograph the courts as a reminder and a thank-you for the freedom you have. Photograph the night sky in the same way.
